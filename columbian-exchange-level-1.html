Words you need

The first four are this unit’s words, and they are the same at every level. The last three are plainer words the reading leans on.

Columbian Exchange
The transfer of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492
Indigenous Peoples
The original inhabitants of a land and their descendants, such as the many Native nations of North America
Colonization
The act of one country taking control of another land and settling people on it
Epidemic
A disease outbreak that spreads rapidly through a population
Continent plain word
One of the world’s big landmasses — Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Australia, Antarctica.
Import plain word
To bring something in from another country to use or sell.
Native to plain word
Naturally from that place, having grown or lived there before anyone brought it.

What the Columbian Exchange was

In 1492, ships from Europe reached the Americas. Before that, the two halves of the world had been apart for thousands of years. After 1492, things began moving between them: plants, animals, people, and diseases. Historians call this the Columbian Exchange.

It was not an even trade. Some things went both ways. Some things went only one way, and that is the part that matters most.

What went out from the Americas

Plants from the Americas went out to the rest of the world. Corn and potatoes were the big ones. They grew in places where older crops failed, so more people could be fed, and the population of Europe climbed.

Tomatoes went to Italy. Cacao, which chocolate is made from, went to Europe. Chili peppers reached India, China and West Africa and changed how people cooked there. None of those foods existed in those places before 1492.

Turkeys went out too. They are the one American animal that people all over the world now keep for food. Tobacco went out as well. Tobacco is not food. It became the crop that colonists in the South grew to sell.

What came in to the Americas

Plants and animals came in to the Americas too. Wheat came for bread. Rice came too, and it was grown in the Carolinas using farming knowledge that enslaved West Africans already had. Coffee came from Africa. Bananas came from Asia. Sugar cane came, and it would matter more than any other plant.

Animals arrived that nobody in the Americas had ever seen: horses, cattle, pigs, chickens. Horses changed life for Native nations on the Plains. Cattle and pigs grazed on land Indigenous people had been farming.

The part that did not balance

Now the part that did not balance.

The ships also carried diseases. Smallpox. Measles. Influenza. People in Europe, Africa and Asia had lived alongside these diseases for centuries. Many had caught them as children and survived, so their bodies knew how to fight them.

Nobody in the Americas had ever met them. Nobody had any protection at all. When smallpox arrived it did not simply make people ill. It killed them, community after community.

Disease did travel the other way as well. Most historians now think syphilis came out of the Americas to Europe after 1492. It spread fast there and it killed people.

But the two sides do not match. Historians argue about the exact numbers. They do not argue about the scale: most Indigenous people in the Americas died in these epidemics. Many died before they ever saw a European face. Nothing that travelled east did harm on anything close to that scale.

So this is not a list of foods. Food moved both ways and both sides gained. Disease moved both ways and only one side was destroyed. That is what made colonization possible.

People were moved too

People crossed the ocean too, and not all of them chose to.

Some Europeans came looking for land or money or a new start. Millions of Africans were taken by force and shipped across the Atlantic to be sold and held as enslaved people. They did not choose to travel, they were not paid, and they could not go home.

So when a book says people were “part of the exchange,” stop on that word. An exchange is a trade, and a trade means both sides agreed. For most of the people who crossed that ocean, nobody asked.

So what?

The Columbian Exchange usually gets taught as a food story. Tomatoes to Italy, chocolate to Europe, horses to the Plains. All of that is true.

But if the food is all you learn, you cannot answer the real question: how did a small number of Europeans end up controlling two continents where millions of people already lived?

The answer is in how unevenly everything else moved.

1. The exchange begins0:56

A name to notice. The video says “the Arawaks.” Arawak is the name of a group of languages, and many different peoples across the Caribbean and South America spoke them. The people of Hispaniola have a more exact name of their own: the Taíno. That is the name to use.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: In 1493, Columbus led a small band of men and 24 horses ashore on the Caribbean island of Hispaniola. The island people, the Arawaks, had seen white-skinned men on Columbus’s first voyage, but they had never before laid eyes on the towering beasts that splashed through the surf that day.

As soon as Columbus arrived in the Americas, worlds that had been separated by vast oceans for millions of years began to merge. This process would transform life on both sides of the Atlantic forever. Sheep, cattle and chickens were all imported into the Americas, as were bananas, coffee and wheat.

And the Americas introduced the world to tomatoes, cocoa, as well as corn and peanuts — foods that now literally feed the world.

2. Horses0:55

Two things the video does not tell you.

Horses were not new to the Americas. They first evolved here. They crossed into Asia and Europe over land that is now under the sea, and then they died out on this side of the world about ten thousand years ago. So the ships in the first video were not bringing the horse to the Americas for the first time. They were bringing it back.

And the buffalo were not “replaced.” Fifteen million of them were still grazing the grasslands in the 1860s. By the 1890s about a thousand were left. Hunters shot them for their hides, mostly in the 1870s and 1880s. It was not only a business, either. Some officials in the government and the army saw a use in it: they believed that if the herds were gone, the army could force Native nations onto reservations. Congress passed a law in 1874 to protect the buffalo. President Grant refused to sign it, so it never took effect.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: Horses frightened the Arawaks when they first saw them, for they believed them to be giant dogs that fed on human flesh. But over time, Native Americans came to use the horse for their own purposes. These dancers of the North American plains lived by hunting buffalo.

But stalking the buffalo on foot was a slow and dangerous affair. Horses revolutionized the hunt. What once took days now took moments. But in time, the buffalo of the plains were replaced by European cattle. With them, a new culture evolved, that of the cowboy.

The all-American rodeo is actually anything but. The bucking bronco, the branded cattle, even the word “rodeo” are foreigners, all Spanish imports.

3. The potato, and what followed it1:23

One word to watch. The video calls the potato blight a fungus. It is not a fungus. It is a water mould, which is a different kind of living thing altogether — closer to algae than to a mushroom. Nobody in 1845 knew that, and the wrong name stuck.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: If European imports like horses and cattle forever changed the Americas, American exports to Europe would forever change life there. The humble potato is one important example. It was grown by peoples of the Andes for hundreds of years before the Spaniards arrived.

Once in Europe, this nutritious vegetable became the food of the poor, and it supported a sharp increase in the European population beginning in the 1600s. Ireland’s population grew more than eightfold. For many, meals consisted of potatoes and nothing else. But then another import from the Americas arrived: a deadly fungus.

By the summer of 1845, potato fields stricken by this fungus blackened overnight. The Irish potato famine had begun. Those who had lived by the potato now died without it. Perhaps a million Irish starved to death. A million others took to ships headed westward across the Atlantic.

They became part of perhaps the most significant cargo to move between the continents: the massive wave of people who would cross the oceans to start a new life in the Americas.
