Driving question

What made independence worth fighting for — and for whom was it left incomplete?

Supporting essential questions

  • How did loyal British subjects turn into revolutionaries in barely a decade?
  • The Declaration says “all men are created equal” — who was actually included, and who was left waiting? (the equity thread that runs through the whole year)
  • How do you decide whether a source — or a podcast host — has earned your trust?
  • What happens when a brand-new nation writes rules too weak to hold it together? (plants the Constitution seed for Unit 3)

Signature project — “Voices of the Revolution” podcast

Student-facing overview

The Revolution you know stars a few famous names. The Revolution that actually happened had thousands of voices — and most of them never made it into the textbook. You'll choose one overlooked Revolutionary figure — maybe the poet Phillis Wheatley, the double agent James Armistead Lafayette, the soldier-in-disguise Deborah Sampson, or the Mohawk leader Joseph Brant — dig into the sources, and build a thesis about why their story matters. Then you'll turn that research into a podcast episode that puts their voice back into the story, using the script builder at voices.mrbsocialstudies.org to plan your episode and cite every source like a real historian. By the end, you'll be able to answer for yourself: independence was worth fighting for — but for whom was it left incomplete?

Teacher notes: the podcast is the year's first full research cycle and locks in the citation habit early. Rubric outcomes to attach in Canvas: ★ SSS4.6-8.1 (thesis & product), ★ SSS4.6-8.2 (citations), and ★ SSS2.6-8.2 (source credibility). H1.6-8.6, H2.6-8.5, H3.6-8.4, C1.6-8.3, C1.6-8.4, and SSS2.6-8.1 ride on smaller tasks (Declaration close read, Patriot/Loyalist perspective work, Articles case study).
