Driving question

How did industry, cities, and immigration remake America — and who built it?

Supporting essential questions

  • How did new technology — railroads, electricity, steel, the telephone — reshape everyday life in a single generation?
  • What did immigrants bring to America, and how did cultural diffusion change what "American" meant? (closes the G2.6-8.7 curriculum gap)
  • Why did the gap between industrialists and workers grow so wide, and what did workers do about it?
  • What pulled millions of people into cities — and what did they find when they got there?

Signature project — "Building Modern America"

Student-facing overview

Between 1877 and 1915, America transformed faster than at any point in its history — railroads stitched the country together, cities shot upward, millions of immigrants arrived, and workers fought for a fair share of it all. Your job: build the museum that tells this story. In teams, you'll curate one wing of our class exhibit — industrialization, urban life, immigration, or labor — choosing the artifacts, writing the captions, and making an argument with your choices. Because here's the thing every real museum wrestles with: the story changes depending on whose stuff you put in the case. A railroad tycoon's pocket watch and a Chinese railroad worker's pickaxe tell very different versions of the same track. Your wing needs a thesis about who really built modern America — and every artifact you choose is evidence for it.

Teacher notes: the exhibit format makes the "whose voice?" habit physical — curation is perspective selection. Rubric outcomes to attach in Canvas: ★ H2.6-8.7 (technology & ideas shape history), ★ G2.6-8.7 (cultural diffusion — a curriculum gap this unit closes), and ★ E2.6-8.5 (economic systems evidence); H2.6-8.6, G2.6-8.6, and G2.6-8.8 ride on smaller tasks. See the alignment page.

Core content

Five threads, braided: industrialization and new technology (railroads, steel, electricity, the corporation and the assembly line), the growth of cities (urbanization, tenements, skyscrapers, political machines), immigration (Ellis Island and Angel Island, the pull of opportunity and the push-back of nativism), the labor movement (unions, strikes, and the fights at Haymarket and Pullman), and running underneath all of it, the gap between industrialists and workers — the people whose names went on buildings and the people who built them. Keep the driving question's "who built it?" thread visible in every lesson: immigrant and worker perspectives sit alongside the industrialists', not behind them.
