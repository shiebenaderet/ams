The dual job of Unit 1

This unit carries two loads at once. It installs the Operating System — the always-on routines from your Loop Map — and it delivers identity + early-colonial content through the "Whose America?" project. The trick is sequencing: you don't teach all seven routines on day one. Community and the lesson cycle go first; academic routines layer in as there's content to retrieve and discuss. By the end of Week 1 most of the system is running; Weeks 2–3 let students practice it on real history.

"Whose America?" project arc

  1. Week 1 — identity life-map: students map their own name, roots, and story.
  2. Week 2 — widen the lens: contact, the Columbian Exchange, and whose voices the record keeps.
  3. Week 3 — expand into the three colonial regions and the perspectives of Indigenous and enslaved people; synthesize into a "Whose America?" piece.

Doubles as the birthplace of the Classroom Constitution (your co-constructed norms).

Week-by-week day plans

Each day follows the same cycle once it's installed: Do Now → mini-lesson → work → debrief → exit. Tags: NEW ROUTINE installs an operating-system piece · STRUCTURE a spiraling discourse/inquiry move · COMMUNITY a belonging move.

Pacing & flex: This is a 15-day frame for a 3-week unit, but it's built to breathe. Day 10 is an explicit float/reteach day; the project studio time on Days 10, 14 absorbs slippage. If a routine isn't sticking, slow down — a well-installed operating system in Unit 1 pays for itself every week after. The handoff to Unit 2 is deliberate: the cumulative retrieval check and the "whose voice was left out?" bridge carry both the content and the habit forward, where the Socratic Seminar gets introduced on the Declaration of Independence.
