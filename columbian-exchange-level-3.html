What the was

For roughly twelve thousand years, the Americas developed on their own. Different plants were domesticated there. Different animals lived there. Different diseases circulated there. Farmers in Mexico bred maize out of a wild grass; farmers in the Andes bred thousands of varieties of potato. None of it existed anywhere else, and nothing from Europe, Africa or Asia existed here.

In 1492 that ended. Over the following centuries, plants, animals, people and diseases moved across the Atlantic in a continuous traffic that historians call the .

The name is fair; the word “exchange” is not quite. Crops genuinely moved in both directions and both sides gained from them. disease moved almost entirely one way. So did the great majority of the people who crossed. An account of this that balances a positives column against a negatives column has already lost the shape of what happened.

What went out from the Americas

The American crops that mattered most to the rest of the world were the ones that produced the most calories from the least land.

Maize — corn — and potatoes are the clearest cases. A potato field in poor, cold, thin soil yields more food than a wheat field in the same ground, and northern Europe has a great deal of poor, cold, thin soil. Populations that had been limited by how much food their land could produce were suddenly not limited in the same way. Historians link the population growth of eighteenth and nineteenth century Europe directly to American crops, and that growth is part of why Europe had people to send abroad.

Other crops changed culture rather than survival. Tomatoes reached Italy and, within two centuries, became inseparable from its cooking. Cacao reached Spain. Chili peppers traveled with Portuguese traders into India, Southeast Asia, China and West Africa and were absorbed so completely that the cuisines now read as though they had always had them. Tobacco crossed to Europe, found a market, and came back as the cash crop that built colonial Virginia — a plant whose profits would shape who was forced to work the land it grew on. Turkeys went the same way, and are the one American animal that became a farmyard bird on other .

What came in to the Americas

Coming the other way were the staples of European agriculture and, more consequentially, its livestock.

Wheat, barley, rice, coffee, bananas and sugar cane all arrived. Coffee is Africa and bananas to Asia, though both are now so ordinary in the Americas that students routinely assume they originated here. Sugar cane deserves its own line: of every plant that crossed westward, it did the most damage, because sugar was enormously profitable and enormously labor-intensive.

The animals were the larger shock. The Americas had no horses, cattle, pigs, sheep or chickens. Horses reached the Great Plains and remade the nations there — hunting, warfare, trade and distance all changed within a few generations, and the mounted Plains cultures that later generations treat as ancient are in fact a product of the Exchange. Cattle and pigs were a different matter. Livestock grazed and rooted through fields nations had cleared and planted, and because colonists let animals range freely, escaped herds frequently reached a region years before any colonist did.

The part that did not balance

The uneven traffic is the mechanism, and it is where this reading stops being a list.

The ships carried smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus and others. Europe, Africa and Asia had been trading, invading and living alongside each other and their livestock for millennia, and disease had circulated among them the whole time. Populations exposed for that long carry survivors, and survivors carry immunity; children who live through an illness are adults who will not die of it.

Nothing in the Americas had that history. There was no prior exposure and therefore no acquired immunity, at any age, anywhere. When smallpox entered a community it did not move through it the way an illness moves through a school, taking a few at a time. It took nearly everyone at once — including the adults who farmed, hunted, healed, governed and remembered. A community can survive losing a share of its people. It has much more trouble surviving the simultaneous loss of the generation that knows how everything is done.

Estimates of the death toll vary widely among historians, and the variation is genuine rather than careless: the sources are thin, the populations were not counted, and reconstructing them involves assumptions reasonable scholars dispute. What is not disputed is the order of magnitude. These killed the majority of the population of the Americas. Many nations lost enormous numbers before meeting a European, because disease moved along trade routes faster than colonists walked.

Disease did move east as well, and the clearest case is syphilis. Europe’s first documented of it breaks out in the 1490s, and ancient DNA recovered from remains in Brazil, Mexico and Colombia now points to treponemal disease having a long history in the Americas before contact. It is the strongest counterexample to any claim that the traffic ran one way, and it killed Europeans for centuries.

Hold all of it together, because the shape matters more than the direction. Crops moved both ways and both sides gained. Disease moved both ways too — and the consequences did not resemble each other at all. One side lost the majority of its population to against which it had no prior exposure. The other lost a great many people to a serious disease. Those are not the same event, and no amount of tomatoes balances the difference.

That gap — not a one-way door — is what made large-scale possible.

People were moved too

People were moved as well, and the direction of that movement was nearly as lopsided as the disease.

Europeans crossed for land, profit, or the freedom to worship as they chose. Africans crossed in far greater numbers and almost entirely by force: captured, sold, packed aboard ships and delivered to plantations as enslaved people, overwhelmingly to grow sugar and tobacco.

Follow the loop, because it is the point. The Exchange carries sugar cane west. Sugar turns out to be immensely profitable. Producing it demands brutal labor at a scale no free worker will supply. The demand for that labor drives the Atlantic slave trade, which moves millions of people west against their will. The movement of a plant and the movement of people are not two separate facts in the same chapter; one produced the other.

This is also why “exchange” needs watching as a word. An exchange is a transaction between parties who both agree to it. Applied to crops it is accurate. Applied to the people in the holds of those ships it is a euphemism, and euphemism is how uncomfortable history gets made comfortable.

The part the story usually leaves out

Three things a tidy version of this story tends to drop.

The land looked empty because it had been emptied. Disease traveled along trade routes, which were extensive and well used, and it therefore ran ahead of the colonists rather than behind them. A great many Europeans arrived in places where the dying had already happened — and concluded, reasonably enough from what was in front of them, that the Americas had always been thinly settled. Plymouth is the case to hold on to. The Pilgrims did not clear that ground. They settled the site of Patuxet, a Wampanoag town whose people had been killed by an in the years just before the Mayflower arrived, and they built on ground that had already been cleared and farmed. Some of them read that as providence prepared for them. It was an . The idea of an empty , which later generations used to justify taking more of it, begins in this gap between when the disease arrived and when the witnesses did.

The numbers are contested, and knowing why is part of knowing the history. Estimates of the pre-1492 population of the Americas range from roughly 8 million to over 100 million, and estimates of mortality vary accordingly. That spread is not sloppiness. Nobody was counting. Reconstructions depend on archaeology, on colonial records written by people with reasons to over- or under-report, and on assumptions about how many people a given landscape can feed. Historians argue about those assumptions in public, which is what a healthy discipline looks like. Be suspicious of any source that gives you one confident number with no range and no explanation of where it came from — and notice that the size of the estimate tends to track what the writer wants the story to mean.

The Exchange did not end; it changed shape. The crops that crossed westward created an agricultural system — sugar, then tobacco, then cotton — whose profits depended on labor that had to be compelled. That system is the direct link between this week and next: the triangular trade you will study is the running as a business, with ships moving manufactured goods, enslaved people and plantation crops around one Atlantic circuit. When you meet it, you will already know why the demand existed.

So what?

Presented as a food story, the is genuinely delightful, and everything in it is true. Tomatoes did reach Italy. Chocolate did reach Europe. Horses did reach the Plains.

But a food story cannot answer the question the unit is actually asking. Millions of people lived in the Americas in 1491, in nations with governments, farms, borders and long histories. Within a few centuries, a far smaller number of newcomers controlled the land. How?

Not by being better armed, and not by being more numerous, because they were neither. The answer is in the asymmetry: crops both ways to everyone’s benefit, disease both ways to wildly unequal effect, people one way in chains. When somebody hands you a tidy list of what the Exchange gave the world, the useful question is what the list leaves out — and whether it left it out by accident.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: In 1493, Columbus led a small band of men and 24 horses ashore on the Caribbean island of Hispaniola. The island people, the Arawaks, had seen white-skinned men on Columbus’s first voyage, but they had never before laid eyes on the towering beasts that splashed through the surf that day.

As soon as Columbus arrived in the Americas, worlds that had been separated by vast oceans for millions of years began to merge. This process would transform life on both sides of the Atlantic forever. Sheep, cattle and chickens were all imported into the Americas, as were bananas, coffee and wheat.

And the Americas introduced the world to tomatoes, cocoa, as well as corn and peanuts — foods that now literally feed the world.

2. Horses0:55

Two things the video does not tell you.

Horses were not new to the Americas. They first evolved here. They crossed into Asia and Europe over land that is now under the sea, and then they died out on this side of the world about ten thousand years ago. So the ships in the first video were not bringing the horse to the Americas for the first time. They were bringing it back.

And the buffalo were not “replaced.” Fifteen million of them were still grazing the grasslands in the 1860s. By the 1890s about a thousand were left. Hunters shot them for their hides, mostly in the 1870s and 1880s. It was not only a business, either. Some officials in the government and the army saw a use in it: they believed that if the herds were gone, the army could force Native nations onto reservations. Congress passed a law in 1874 to protect the buffalo. President Grant refused to sign it, so it never took effect.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: Horses frightened the Arawaks when they first saw them, for they believed them to be giant dogs that fed on human flesh. But over time, Native Americans came to use the horse for their own purposes. These dancers of the North American plains lived by hunting buffalo.

But stalking the buffalo on foot was a slow and dangerous affair. Horses revolutionized the hunt. What once took days now took moments. But in time, the buffalo of the plains were replaced by European cattle. With them, a new culture evolved, that of the cowboy.

The all-American rodeo is actually anything but. The bucking bronco, the branded cattle, even the word “rodeo” are foreigners, all Spanish imports.

3. The potato, and what followed it1:23

One word to watch. The video calls the potato blight a fungus. It is not a fungus. It is a water mold, which is a different kind of living thing altogether — closer to algae than to a mushroom. Nobody in 1845 knew that, and the wrong name stuck.

What the narrator says

NARRATOR: If European imports like horses and cattle forever changed the Americas, American exports to Europe would forever change life there. The humble potato is one important example. It was grown by peoples of the Andes for hundreds of years before the Spaniards arrived.

Once in Europe, this nutritious vegetable became the food of the poor, and it supported a sharp increase in the European population beginning in the 1600s. Ireland’s population grew more than eightfold. For many, meals consisted of potatoes and nothing else. But then another import from the Americas arrived: a deadly fungus.

By the summer of 1845, potato fields stricken by this fungus blackened overnight. The Irish potato famine had begun. Those who had lived by the potato now died without it. Perhaps a million Irish starved to death. A million others took to ships headed westward across the Atlantic.

They became part of perhaps the most significant cargo to move between the continents: the massive wave of people who would cross the oceans to start a new life in the Americas.
