The Three Colonial Regions

Words you need

The same words at every level. The last three belong to one region each.

Cash crop
A plant grown to sell for money, not to eat.
Subsistence farming
Growing just enough food to feed your own family.
Indentured servant
Someone who worked for about 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip across the ocean. Then they were free.
Enslaved
Forced to work for no pay, with no right to leave. Enslaved people were treated as property that could be bought and sold.
Export
Something a place sells to another country.
Self-government
People making their own local rules instead of taking orders from far away.
Puritan New England
A member of a religious group that wanted to live exactly by the Bible.
Tolerance Middle
Letting people believe and worship differently from you.
Plantation Southern
A very large farm that grew one crop to sell, worked by enslaved people.

New England Colonies

Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire

The land and the weather

The New England colonies were Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Hampshire. Start with the ground, because the ground decided a lot. New England’s soil is thin, rocky and hard to plow. Glaciers (huge sheets of slow-moving ice) scraped across it during the last ice age and left the stones behind, which is why the region is full of stone walls: farmers had to pull rocks out of a field before they could plant, so they stacked them at the edges.

The winters are long and cold and the growing season is short. Tobacco and rice need months of heat, so neither would grow here. What New England did have was a jagged coastline (the edge of the land where it meets the sea) full of deep harbors (sheltered water deep enough for ships to anchor safely), and forests of tall straight trees running right down to the water.

How people made money

Because nobody was going to get rich farming, New Englanders turned to the sea. Three things made the money.

Fishing came first. The waters off the coast were thick with cod. Fishermen dried and salted it so it would keep, then sold it across the Atlantic and in the Caribbean. Shipbuilding came second: the forests gave them timber, and New England ships became famous for their quality and were sold all over the Atlantic world. Trade came third. Boston, Salem and Newport grew into busy ports where merchants brought goods in and sent fish, timber and rum out.

Families still farmed, but mostly to feed themselves, growing corn, beans and squash. That is subsistence (growing just enough to feed your own family) farming, and it is the opposite of a cash crop. It keeps you alive. It does not make you wealthy.

Who did the work

Most work in New England was done by the family that owned the land, plus their children. Communities worked together at harvest and for big jobs like building a barn. Some workers were indentured servants who had traded a few years of labor for the voyage over.

New England is often described as the region without slavery. That is not true. A smaller share of people were enslaved than in the South, but enslaved people lived and worked in every New England colony. And New England merchants grew wealthy on trade that ran on slavery: their ships carried enslaved people, and their distilleries turned Caribbean molasses, grown by enslaved people, into rum.

Who settled here, and why

Most New England colonists came for religion. The Puritans (English Protestants who wanted a stricter, purer church) wanted to worship as they believed the Bible required, which they could not do in England. They came as whole families rather than as single young men looking for money, and that made their towns grow quickly and stay put.

Religion was not a private matter here. Every town had a church at its center, attendance was expected, and ministers had real influence over the rules. That unity had a sharp edge: when Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson disagreed with Puritan leaders, they were forced out of Massachusetts. Williams founded Rhode Island, which did allow different beliefs.

Who made the rules

Britain appointed a governor for each colony, but New England towns handled most local business themselves at town meetings, where men who owned property voted on roads, schools and taxes.

The Mayflower Compact of 1620 came earlier and mattered later. Before stepping off the ship, the Pilgrims signed an agreement to make “just and equal laws” together. It is a small document, but it set a precedent: a government that exists because the governed agreed to it. That is self-government.

So what?

New England’s geography closed one door and opened another. Rocky soil and short summers ruled out the cash crops that made other regions rich, so New Englanders built an economy out of fish, timber, ships and trade. Their religion shaped their towns, and their town meetings built habits of self-government that would matter enormously a century later.

Middle Colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware

The land and the weather

The Middle Colonies were New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware, and almost everything about them is explained by the word “between.” They sat between New England and the South, and their climate did too: warm summers, real winters, and a growing season long enough to be generous without being tropical.

The land was the best farmland on the coast — deep, fertile soil, especially in the river valleys. And two great rivers, the Hudson and the Delaware, cut from that farmland straight out to the Atlantic. That combination is the whole story. Good soil with no way to move the harvest makes a comfortable farmer. Good soil with a navigable river makes an export economy.

How people made money

The Middle Colonies grew grain, and they grew it in quantities that earned them the nickname “the breadbasket of America.” Wheat was the leader, with rye, oats and barley behind it. Families ate part of the harvest and sold the rest, so grain worked as a cash crop even though it was also food.

Farming was not the only thing happening. Ironworks smelted ore into tools. Shipyards built vessels. Lumber mills cut timber. Artisans (skilled workers who made things by hand) — skilled workers who made things by hand — turned out shoes, furniture, barrels and cloth for a growing population. And two of the busiest ports in North America, New York City and Philadelphia, moved all of it. Philadelphia would become the largest city in the colonies.

Who did the work

Labor here was mixed, which is worth noticing, because labor is usually where the regions differ most.

Most farms were family farms worked by the family, often with hired hands paid wages at harvest. A very large number of colonists arrived as indentured servants, particularly Germans and Irish, trading four to seven years of work for the crossing; when their term ended they were free, and in a region with land available that freedom could turn into a farm.

Slavery existed here too. New York City in particular had a substantial enslaved population working in households, on docks and in workshops. But the Middle Colonies never developed plantations, and the reason is agricultural: wheat needs intense labor for a few weeks at harvest and comparatively little the rest of the year. Rice and tobacco need constant labor for months. The crop shapes the labor system.

Who settled here, and why

No other region looked like this one. Settlers came from England, the Netherlands, Germany, Sweden, Scotland and Ireland, and they brought their languages and their churches with them: Quakers (a religious group who refused to fight in wars), Lutherans, Mennonites, Presbyterians, Catholics, Jews and more.

Pennsylvania is the clearest case. William Penn, a Quaker, founded it as a place where belief would not be punished, and a 1701 law guaranteed that anyone who acknowledged God could worship freely. Penn also insisted on buying land from the Lenape rather than seizing it. Tolerance was written into law and it did attract people — though tolerance is not the same thing as equality, and Penn’s sons did not keep his promises.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor tied to British authority and an elected assembly of colonists that passed local laws and controlled local money. Pennsylvania’s assembly was unusually powerful. Colonists here had genuine practice at self-government, and the diversity of the population meant assemblies (groups of elected men who made laws for a colony) had to accommodate people who disagreed with each other.

So what?

The Middle Colonies got the best of both neighbors. Their soil and growing season let them farm for profit like the South; their rivers and harbors let them trade and build cities like New England. Because they did not depend on a crop that required an enormous permanent workforce, they did not build their society around slavery the way the South did. And because they welcomed people other colonies turned away, they became the most diverse society in British North America.

Southern Colonies

Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia

The land and the weather

The Southern Colonies were Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia, and their geography handed them an advantage the other regions did not have. Summers were hot and long, winters were mild, and rain was plentiful. The growing season ran for most of the year.

The land was flat coastal plain with rich soil, especially in the tidewater — the low country near the coast where wide, slow rivers reach the sea. Those rivers were the second advantage. They were deep enough for ocean-going ships, so a planter did not need a town or a road. A ship could come to his dock, load the crop and sail for England.

How people made money

Agriculture was the Southern economy, but the important distinction is between farming to eat and farming to sell. Small farmers did plenty of subsistence farming. The wealth, though, came from plantations: very large farms that grew a single cash crop for export.

Tobacco led in Virginia and Maryland, and it was so valuable that colonists sometimes used it in place of money. Rice took hold in the marshy low country of South Carolina and Georgia — and the technique came from West Africa, where enslaved people had grown rice for generations before they were brought here. Indigo (a plant used to make deep blue dye), which produces a deep blue dye that European cloth makers wanted badly, grew alongside it. Almost all of it was exported (sold and shipped out to another country).

Who did the work

Here is the connection this whole reading is built around. Tobacco and rice are not like wheat. They need attention nearly every month: seeding, transplanting, weeding, topping, worming, harvesting, drying, packing. A crop that demands year-round labor makes a large permanent workforce profitable, and Southern planters went looking for one.

In the early 1600s they used indentured servants, mostly young English men and women who traded four to seven years of unpaid work for the voyage. Indenture had an end date; survivors went free and some got land. Across the later 1600s planters shifted to enslaved Africans instead. Enslavement had no end date and no contract. Enslaved people were held as property, could be sold away from their families, and by law their children inherited the same status. By the mid-1700s enslaved people were the majority of the population in South Carolina.

Who settled here, and why

The Southern colonies were not founded for religious reasons the way New England was. Most settlers came for land, for profit, or to escape debt. Maryland is the partial exception: it was founded as a refuge for English Catholics, though Protestants soon outnumbered them. The Church of England was the official church in most Southern colonies, but religious life was looser than in Puritan New England.

Enslaved Africans did not choose to come. They were captured, sold, and carried across the Atlantic by force, and by the 1700s they and their descendants were a huge share of the Southern population. Any sentence that starts “people came to the South because...” has to account for the people who did not come, but were brought.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor representing British authority and an elected assembly that made local law. Virginia’s House of Burgesses, founded in 1619, was the first elected assembly in English America.

But look at who sat in it. Voting and officeholding required property, so assemblies were dominated by wealthy planters, and the laws they passed protected planter interests — including the slave codes that made enslavement permanent, hereditary (passed down from parent to child, permanently) and racial. Self-government in the South was real, and it was self-government for a small group of men at the top.

So what?

The South’s climate and soil made cash crops possible, and cash crops made enormous wealth. But cash crops on that scale demanded labor all year long, and planters met that demand by enslaving people. The environment created the opportunity; human beings chose how to take it. That distinction is the point of this whole unit: geography opens doors, and people decide what to do once they walk through.
