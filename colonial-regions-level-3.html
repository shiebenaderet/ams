The Three Colonial Regions

Level 3 — The long way round, with one extra section on what the tidy version leaves out.

Words you need

The same words at every level. The last three belong to one region each.

Cash crop
A crop grown to be sold for profit rather than eaten by the family that grew it. Tobacco, rice and indigo were cash crops.
Subsistence farming
Farming that produces enough to feed the household, with little or nothing left to sell. The opposite of growing a cash crop.
Indentured servant
A person who signed a contract to work without wages for a set number of years, usually four to seven, in exchange for passage to America. At the end of the term they were free, and the contract had an end date.
Enslaved
Held as property and forced to work without pay, for life, with that status passed to one’s children. Unlike indenture, it had no end date and no contract, and by law it was inherited.
Export
A good sent out of a colony to be sold somewhere else. What a region exports tells you what its land and labor could produce.
Self-government
A community making its own laws rather than receiving them from a distant authority. Every colony had some of it, and every colony also had a governor answering to Britain.
Puritan New England
An English Protestant who wanted to “purify” the Church of England. Puritans founded Massachusetts as a community run on their own religious rules.
Tolerance Middle
Allowing beliefs and practices you disagree with. In the Middle Colonies it was written into law, which is not the same as everyone getting along.
Plantation Southern
A large agricultural estate producing a single cash crop for export, worked by a concentrated labor force — by the 1700s, almost entirely enslaved Africans and their descendants.

New England Colonies

Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire

The land and the weather

The New England colonies — Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island and New Hampshire — occupied land that set hard limits. Glaciation (the long period when sheets of ice covered the land) had stripped the region’s soil thin and seeded it with stone; the growing season ran about five months; and no crop that could be sold at a profit in Europe would tolerate the winters. Against that, the coast offered an unusual concentration of deep natural harbors (sheltered water deep enough for ships to anchor safely), and mature white pine and oak forests stood within hauling distance of them.

The useful way to read this is not that New England was poor in resources but that it was rich in the wrong ones for agriculture and exactly right for shipping. The region’s entire economic shape follows from which resources happened to be valuable in an Atlantic market.

How people made money

New England’s economy faced outward, toward water. Cod from the offshore banks (shallow places far out at sea where fish gather — not banks for money) was salted and dried into a commodity (something bought and sold in bulk, like wheat or sugar) durable enough to cross an ocean; the best grades went to Catholic southern Europe and the worst, called “refuse fish,” went to Caribbean sugar islands as cheap food for enslaved workers. Timber fed a shipbuilding industry so productive that by the mid-eighteenth century a large share of British merchant shipping was New England-built. Merchants in Boston, Salem, Newport and Providence handled the exchange, importing British manufactured goods and exporting fish, timber, livestock and rum.

Household agriculture remained subsistence farming — corn, rye, beans, squash, enough to feed a family and little more. The contrast with a cash crop economy is the thing to hold on to. A cash crop concentrates wealth in whoever controls land and labor. A carrying trade concentrates wealth in whoever controls ships and credit. Both produce inequality; they produce different kinds.

Who did the work

Because no New England crop demanded a large permanent workforce, labor was organized around households: family members, apprentices (young people learning a trade by working for someone who had it) bound to a trade, and indentured servants working off a passage under a contract that expired. Wage labor was comparatively common, particularly on the waterfront.

Enslaved people were nonetheless present in every New England colony, concentrated in ports and in wealthy households, and in Newport they formed a significant fraction of the population. The deeper entanglement was commercial. Rhode Island merchants financed, outfitted and insured slaving voyages on a scale that made Newport one of the major slave-trading ports in British North America. New England distilleries (places where molasses was boiled and turned into rum) bought Caribbean molasses (thick dark syrup left over when sugar cane is boiled down) — the product of enslaved labor — and converted it into rum that was then exchanged on the West African coast for captives (people taken and held against their will). The absence of plantations in New England is a fact about its soil, not about its conscience (your own sense of right and wrong).

Who settled here, and why

New England’s founding population was religiously motivated and demographically unusual. Puritans emigrated in families and whole congregations (the people who worshipped together at one church) rather than as unattached young men, producing a balanced sex ratio, rapid natural increase and stable towns — conditions the Chesapeake would not reach for generations. John Winthrop’s “city upon a hill” was not a metaphor about opportunity; it was a claim that the community would be judged, collectively, on whether it kept its covenant (a binding promise, treated as sacred).

A community defined by shared belief handles disagreement badly. Roger Williams was banished (forced to leave and never come back) in 1636 for two arguments the colony could not absorb: that civil government has no authority over conscience, and that the king could not grant land that belonged to the Narragansett. Anne Hutchinson was tried and expelled in 1638 for teaching that salvation came through grace rather than visible obedience. Rhode Island exists as the residue of Massachusetts’s intolerance.

Who made the rules

Royal authority was real but thin on the ground. What filled the space was the town meeting, in which adult male property holders voted directly on taxation, roads, schooling and the hiring of ministers. Few people anywhere in the eighteenth-century world had that much routine practice at governing themselves, and that practice is a large part of why resistance to British policy in the 1760s organized so quickly in this region.

The Mayflower Compact of 1620 is best read as precedent rather than achievement. It bound roughly forty men, excluded women, servants and the Wampanoag on whose cleared land the colony was planted, and created no democracy. Its significance is the sentence underneath it: that the authority to make “just and equal laws” came from the agreement of the people bound by them.

The part the story usually leaves out

The standard version of this region’s story is adaptation: colonists met a hard land and cleverly turned to the sea. Two pieces of evidence complicate it.

The first is that the land was not empty, and much of it was not wild. The Wampanoag, Massachusett, Narragansett and Pequot had farmed, burned and managed this ground for centuries; the fields the Pilgrims planted at Patuxet in 1621 were already cleared, by people an epidemic had killed between 1616 and 1619. The “wilderness” that Puritan writers described conquering was in significant part a graveyard. Expansion then produced war: the Pequot War of 1636-37 and King Philip’s War of 1675-76, the second of which killed a larger share of New England’s population than any later American war and broke Native power in southern New England permanently.

The second is the rum. It is easy to file New England under “small farms, no plantations, therefore no slavery.” But Newport’s wealth, Boston’s shipping and the distilleries that made rum from Caribbean molasses were all positions inside the slave economy. A region can profit from a system without hosting it. Noticing that is the difference between describing a map and reading one.

So what?

New England demonstrates environment and human choice acting on each other in both directions. Thin soil and deep harbors pushed the colonies toward fishing, shipbuilding and carrying trade rather than staple agriculture; that economy concentrated population in ports, produced a merchant elite, and made the region a node in an Atlantic system built on enslaved labor even though few enslaved people worked New England fields. Meanwhile the colonists reshaped the environment they had entered — clearing forest, fencing commons, and displacing the nations whose management had produced the cleared land in the first place. Both directions of that exchange belong in your notes.

Middle Colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware

The land and the weather

The Middle Colonies — New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware — held the most agriculturally favorable position on the British North American coast: a temperate climate with a growing season of roughly six months, deep fertile soils in the Hudson, Delaware and Susquehanna valleys, and navigable (deep and wide enough for ships to sail along) rivers connecting that interior farmland directly to protected deep-water harbors.

The analytical point is the connection rather than either half of it. Fertile land without transport supports subsistence farming and local surplus (more than you need, so you can sell the rest); fertile land with river access to an Atlantic port supports commercial agriculture at scale. The Middle Colonies had the second arrangement, and their economy followed.

How people made money

Cereal agriculture anchored the regional economy. Wheat, rye, oats and barley moved out through Philadelphia and New York as grain, flour and bread, bound for New England, the Caribbean sugar islands and southern Europe — the trade that earned the region its nickname, “the breadbasket of America.” Grain sits awkwardly across the categories we use: it functioned as a cash crop while also feeding the household that raised it, which is precisely why it produced a broad class of moderately prosperous farmers instead of a narrow planter elite.

Diversification went further than agriculture. Pennsylvania’s ironworks made it one of the British Empire’s significant iron producers. Shipyards, gristmills (water-powered mills that ground grain into flour), sawmills, tanneries and distilleries clustered along the waterways. Artisans (skilled workers who made things by hand) supplied urban demand. By 1760 Philadelphia was the largest city in British North America and a center of printing, science and finance. The region had something neither neighbor did: an economy that would not collapse if one commodity’s price fell.

Who did the work

Labor arrangements in the Middle Colonies were more varied than anywhere else on the coast, and the variation is explained by crop cycles rather than by regional virtue. Wheat demands concentrated labor for a few weeks at harvest and comparatively little otherwise, which makes seasonal wage labor efficient and a large permanent workforce a poor investment. Tobacco and rice demand sustained labor across most of the year, which is the condition under which purchasing people outright becomes profitable.

So the region ran on family labor, hired hands, and indentured servitude on a very large scale — German and Scots-Irish migrants in particular financed passage by contracting four to seven years of unpaid work. Because land remained obtainable, a surviving servant could realistically become a landowner, which is the structural difference between indenture and slavery: indenture had an end date and did not pass to your children.

Slavery was nevertheless present and locally important. New York City held one of the largest urban enslaved populations in British North America, enslaved labor worked Hudson Valley estates, and New York’s slave codes were severe — the repression following the 1712 revolt and the 1741 conspiracy trials was brutal. “Less slavery than the South” is a statement about proportion, not about absence.

Who settled here, and why

The Middle Colonies assembled the most heterogeneous population in British North America: English, Dutch, German, Swedish, Scots-Irish and Scottish settlers, and Quaker, Lutheran, Mennonite, Dutch Reformed, Presbyterian, Anglican, Catholic and Jewish congregations. This was the product of policy. William Penn received Pennsylvania in 1681 and organized it on the Quaker principle of the inner light, which makes religious coercion (being made to do something by force or threat) not merely cruel but incoherent; the 1701 Charter of Privileges guaranteed that no believer in God would be “molested or prejudiced” for their practice.

Tolerance was also an economic strategy. Penn advertised in German-speaking Europe and sold land to people other colonies would not take, and the population and capital that arrived made Philadelphia the largest city on the continent within eighty years. Both things are true at once: a genuine religious conviction and a land-sales prospectus.

Who made the rules

Governors represented external authority; elected assemblies (groups of elected men who made laws for a colony) held the power that mattered day to day, including the power to tax. Pennsylvania’s assembly became strong enough to constrain the proprietors who nominally owned the colony.

What distinguishes self-government here is the composition of the governed. An assembly answerable to Quakers, Lutherans, Presbyterians and Anglicans at once could not legislate by assuming a common creed, and had to build procedures for disagreement instead. That is a different political education from a New England town meeting among co-religionists, and a different one again from a Virginia assembly of planters who mostly agreed with each other about what mattered.

The part the story usually leaves out

The Middle Colonies are usually taught as the tolerant region, and the evidence for that is real. Two things complicate it.

First, tolerance had edges. Pennsylvania’s guarantee covered people who acknowledged God; officeholding in practice required Protestant Christianity. Catholics and Jews could worship but not govern. And the region’s openness to European migrants coexisted with slave codes in New York severe enough that after the 1741 conspiracy trials the colony executed more than thirty people, most of them Black, on evidence that would not survive a first-year law class.

Second, Penn’s peace did not survive his family. Penn purchased land from the Lenape by treaty and the arrangement held while he lived. In 1737 his sons produced a supposed old deed entitling them to as much land as a man could walk in a day and a half, then hired trained runners and cleared a path ahead of them. The Walking Purchase took roughly 1.2 million acres and pushed the Lenape out of the Delaware valley. The region’s reputation was earned by one generation and spent by the next, which is a useful thing to know before you write “tolerant” in a box.

So what?

The Middle Colonies show environment setting options and people choosing among them. Soil, climate and rivers made both commercial farming and urban trade possible, and the region took both, producing the most diversified and most broadly prosperous economy on the coast. The crop mix made a plantation labor system unprofitable, so the region built itself on family farms, wages and indenture — a structural explanation, not a moral one. Deliberate policies of toleration then drew a population no other colony had, and the limits of that toleration, in New York’s slave codes and in the Walking Purchase, mark exactly where the region’s principles stopped and its interests began.

Southern Colonies

Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia

The land and the weather

The Southern Colonies — Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia — held the most commercially favorable agricultural geography in British North America. A humid subtropical (almost tropical: long hot wet summers, mild winters) climate produced long hot summers, mild winters, abundant rainfall and a growing season roughly twice New England’s. A wide flat coastal plain carried deep fertile soils, and tidal (rising and falling twice a day with the ocean) rivers remained navigable for ocean-going vessels far inland.

One structural consequence deserves attention because it shaped Southern society for two centuries: navigable rivers reaching individual estates removed the need for port cities. Chesapeake planters shipped from their own wharves. Wealth, population and political power therefore dispersed across plantations rather than concentrating in towns, which is why the South produced few cities, fewer schools and a thinner artisan class than a region with equal wealth otherwise would.

How people made money

The Southern economy was organized around export cash crops, though the typical white Southerner was a small farmer doing subsistence farming rather than a planter. Wealth and power were concentrated at the top, and the crops explain where.

Tobacco dominated the Chesapeake to the point of functioning as currency, and Virginia statute (a written law) actively directed its cultivation. Rice remade the Carolina and Georgia low country after the 1690s, and the technical knowledge behind it came from West Africa: planters specifically sought captives from the rice-growing regions of Senegambia and Sierra Leone and paid premium prices for them, which is one of the clearest available demonstrations that enslaved people contributed expertise, not merely muscle, to the economy that held them. Indigo (a plant used to make deep blue dye) became viable after 1748 when Parliament paid a bounty (a cash reward a government paid to get people to grow something) on it, an instructive reminder that imperial policy, not soil alone, decided what was worth planting.

Who did the work

The labor system follows from the crop calendar. Tobacco requires attention for roughly ten months — seedbeds in winter, transplanting, repeated weeding, topping, worming, cutting, curing (drying tobacco leaves slowly in a barn so they keep), stripping, prizing into hogsheads. Rice required constructing and maintaining an engineered landscape of banks, ditches and tidal floodgates, work done standing in water in a malarial environment. Crops like these make a large permanent labor force profitable. Wheat does not. That difference, not any difference in the character of the colonists, is the origin of the divergence between the Southern labor system and the Middle Colonies'.

Through most of the seventeenth century the Chesapeake met the demand with indentured servants: tens of thousands of young English migrants trading four to seven years of unpaid labor for passage, under conditions that killed a large share of them before their terms expired. Indenture was nevertheless contractual, finite and not racial.

The transition to chattel (treated as property that could be bought, sold and inherited) slavery is legible in the statutes, and reading them in order is the best way to see that the system was constructed rather than inherited. Virginia 1662: a child’s status follows the mother’s, making slavery hereditary (passed down from parent to child, permanently). Virginia 1667: baptism does not confer freedom, closing the Christian exit. Virginia 1691: severe penalties for interracial marriage, and restrictions on manumission. Virginia 1705: the slave codes consolidate enslaved people as property, permanently, by race, with violence against them legally protected. Each of these is a decision made by an assembly of men who could have decided otherwise.

Who settled here, and why

Southern settlement was driven by economic opportunity rather than religious mission. Migrants sought land, profit and advancement; Georgia was chartered (given official permission by the king to exist) in 1732 partly as a debtors (people who owed money they could not pay back)' refuge and, notably, initially banned slavery before its settlers successfully lobbied to legalize it in 1751 — a useful case, because it shows the system being chosen deliberately and against an existing prohibition. Maryland began in 1634 as a Catholic refuge and passed the 1649 Act Concerning Religion, one of the earliest toleration statutes in the colonies, which a Protestant majority later repealed.

The largest migration to the colonial South was involuntary. Africans were captured, sold and transported by force; by the mid-eighteenth century people of African descent constituted around forty percent of Virginia’s population and a clear majority of South Carolina’s. Treating “who settled here and why” as a question only about Europeans reproduces the erasure the sources already perform.

Who made the rules

Royal or proprietary (owned by one man the king had handed the land to) governors held formal authority; elected assemblies held practical power, and Virginia’s House of Burgesses (1619) was the first representative assembly in English America. Southern self-government was old, genuine and narrow: property qualifications restricted the franchise, planter elites dominated the assemblies, and the legislation those assemblies produced advanced planter interests.

The uncomfortable part is that the same institutions did both jobs. The Virginia assembly that developed the vocabulary of consent, liberty and representation is the Virginia assembly that wrote the 1705 slave codes. These were not competing traditions; they were the same men in the same session.

The part the story usually leaves out

The tidy version of this region says: warm climate, cash crops, therefore slavery. The climate part is true and the therefore is not. Three pieces of evidence show the system being built by decisions.

Bacon’s Rebellion, 1676. Nathaniel Bacon led a force of poor whites and enslaved and indentured Black Virginians against the colonial government, and they burned Jamestown. The rebellion terrified the planter class less because of Bacon than because poor people of both races had made common cause. The legislation that followed over the next three decades widened the legal gap between poor whites and enslaved Blacks, extending small privileges to the former while stripping the latter. Historians argue about how direct the causation was, but the sequence is documented and the incentive is plain.

Georgia, 1735 to 1751. The colony’s trustees prohibited slavery outright. Settlers campaigned for sixteen years to overturn the ban and won. A prohibition that has to be repealed is not a natural outcome; it is a policy someone reversed.

The Stono Rebellion, 1739. Around sixty enslaved people near the Stono River in South Carolina seized weapons and marched toward Spanish Florida, which had promised freedom to those who reached it. It was put down, and the colony responded with the 1740 Negro Act restricting movement, assembly, education and manumission. Resistance is part of the record, and so is the tightening that answered it.

If you can hold these three alongside the climate, you are doing the thing the fourth level of this outcome asks for: explaining how the environment shaped people and how people shaped what happened next.

So what?

The Southern colonies demonstrate environmental opportunity and human decision operating together, and the unit’s central argument lives here. Climate, soil and navigable rivers made high-value export agriculture possible. The particular crops that flourished demanded labor across nearly the whole year, which made a permanent workforce profitable in a way it was not further north. But profitability is not compulsion. Planters and the assemblies they controlled built a hereditary, racial slavery step by step in statute, reversed a colony’s ban to extend it, and tightened it further each time enslaved people resisted. Geography set the terms of the problem. People wrote the answer, and then wrote it into law.
