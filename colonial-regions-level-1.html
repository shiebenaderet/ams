Words you need

The same words at every level. The last three belong to one region each.

Cash crop
A plant grown to sell for money, not to eat. Academic: A crop grown to be sold for profit rather than eaten by the family that grew it. Tobacco, rice and indigo were cash crops.
Subsistence farming
Growing just enough food to feed your own family. Academic: Farming that produces enough to feed the household, with little or nothing left to sell. The opposite of growing a cash crop.
Indentured servant
Someone who worked for about 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip across the ocean. Then they were free. Academic: A person who signed a contract to work without wages for a set number of years, usually four to seven, in exchange for passage to America. At the end of the term they were free, and the contract had an end date.
Enslaved
Forced to work for no pay, with no right to leave. Enslaved people were treated as property that could be bought and sold. Academic: Held as property and forced to work without pay, for life, with that status passed to one’s children. Unlike indenture, it had no end date and no contract, and by law it was inherited.
Export
Something a place sells to another country. Academic: A good sent out of a colony to be sold somewhere else. What a region exports tells you what its land and labor could produce.
Self-government
People making their own local rules instead of taking orders from far away. Academic: A community making its own laws rather than receiving them from a distant authority. Every colony had some of it, and every colony also had a governor answering to Britain.
Puritan New England
A member of a religious group that wanted to live exactly by the Bible. Academic: An English Protestant who wanted to “purify” the Church of England. Puritans founded Massachusetts as a community run on their own religious rules.
Tolerance Middle
Letting people believe and worship differently from you. Academic: Allowing beliefs and practices you disagree with. In the Middle Colonies it was written into law, which is not the same as everyone getting along.
Plantation Southern
A very large farm that grew one crop to sell, worked by enslaved people. Academic: A large agricultural estate producing a single cash crop for export, worked by a concentrated labor force — by the 1700s, almost entirely enslaved Africans and their descendants.

New England Colonies

Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire

The land and the weather

The New England colonies were Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Hampshire. The soil there is thin and full of rocks, because glaciers (huge sheets of slow-moving ice) dragged those rocks across the land during the ice age and dropped them when the ice melted. Winters are long and cold, so the growing season is short. That is why no would grow here: the crops that made other colonies rich needed more heat than New England could give them.

But New England had two other things, and in the end both mattered more than good soil would have. Its coast was full of deep, safe harbors (sheltered water deep enough for ships to anchor safely), and its forests were full of tall trees.

How people made money

Since farming could not make them rich, New Englanders made money from the sea instead. They caught cod, dried it, salted it, and sold it to other countries. They cut down trees and built ships, and New England ships became some of the best in the world. Boston became a busy port where goods came in and went out.

New England also made rum. Ships carried molasses (thick dark syrup left over when sugar cane is boiled down) up from the Caribbean, and New England distilleries (places where molasses was boiled and turned into rum) boiled it into rum. Traders then carried that rum to the coast of Africa and exchanged it for captured people. So New England money and slavery were tied together, even though the were somewhere else.

Most families still farmed, but only to feed themselves. They grew corn, beans, and squash. That is . It fed you, but it did not make you rich.

Who did the work

Most of the work was done by the family that owned the farm. Children worked as soon as they were old enough. Neighbors helped each other with big jobs like raising a barn. Some workers were who had traded a few years of work for the trip across the ocean.

People often say New England did not have slavery. That is not true. people lived in every New England colony. What New England did not have was , because no crop here needed hundreds of workers at once. The difference was the size of the farms, not the goodness of the owners.

Who settled here, and why

Most people came to New England for religion. The wanted to worship in their own way and build towns that followed their rules. They came in families, not alone, and that is one reason New England towns grew fast.

But a town built on one set of religious rules does not leave much room to disagree. Roger Williams said the government had no business telling people what to believe, and he said the land really belonged to the Narragansett. Massachusetts forced him out in 1636, so he started Rhode Island. Anne Hutchinson was put on trial and forced out in 1638. Rhode Island exists because Massachusetts pushed people out.

Who made the rules

Britain sent a governor. But New England towns ran most things themselves in town meetings, where men who owned land voted on roads, schools and the minister’s pay.

Before the Pilgrims even left their ship in 1620, they signed the Mayflower Compact and promised to make “just and equal laws” together. It was not a democracy. Women, servants, and the Wampanoag whose land they settled on had no part in it. But it wrote down an idea that mattered later: a government is only fair if the people agree to it.

So what?

New England’s land was bad for farming, so its people turned to the ocean instead. Fish, ships, trade and rum made the money that the soil could not. Their religion shaped how they lived, and it also decided who had to leave. Their town meetings started an idea that would matter later: people making their own rules.

Middle Colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware

The land and the weather

The Middle Colonies were New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware. They sat between cold New England and the hot South, and their weather sat in between too: warm summers, cold winters, and a growing season long enough to grow a lot. The soil was deep and good.

Two big rivers, the Hudson and the Delaware, ran from the farmland out to the ocean. That mattered, because a farmer could load grain on a boat and it would reach a ship without ever going near a road.

How people made money

The Middle Colonies grew so much wheat that people called them the breadbasket (a region that grows most of the grain everyone else eats) of America. Farmers grew wheat, rye, oats and barley. They ate some of it and sold the rest, so grain was both their food and their .

They also made things. People built ships, made iron tools, and cut timber into lumber at sawmills. Skilled workers made shoes, furniture and barrels by hand. New York City and Philadelphia grew into big ports where goods came in and went out.

Who did the work

Most farms here were family farms, and the family did the work. Farmers also hired workers and paid them wages at harvest. Many people came as , especially from Germany and Ireland. They worked a few years to pay for the trip, then they were free, and because land was still available they could often get a farm of their own.

Some people in the Middle Colonies were , especially in New York City. But there were no huge here, because wheat does not need hundreds of workers the way rice does.

Who settled here, and why

People came here from many countries: England, Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden, Scotland and Ireland. They spoke different languages and belonged to different churches. Pennsylvania was started by William Penn, a Quaker. Quakers believed in peace and fairness. A Pennsylvania law in 1701 said anyone who believed in God could worship their own way. That is why so many different people moved there.

But had limits. The law protected Christians, and mostly Protestants. Penn bought land from the Lenape instead of taking it, but his sons did not keep that promise. In 1737 they ran the Walking Purchase, a land deal they rigged to take far more Lenape land than the agreement actually described.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor sent from Britain and an elected assembly of colonists who made local laws. Colonists here had a real say in their own rules, and that is self- government. It was harder work here than in other regions, because the people in one assembly did not all speak the same language or go to the same church.

So what?

The Middle Colonies had good farmland and good ports, so they got both a farming economy and a city economy. Because they welcomed people from many places and many religions, they became the most mixed region in the colonies — though that welcome never reached everyone.

Southern Colonies

Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia

The land and the weather

The Southern Colonies were Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia. The weather there was hot in summer and mild in winter, so the growing season was long and crops could grow for most of the year. The land was flat and the soil was rich.

Wide, slow rivers ran through it out to the ocean. A ship could sail up one of those rivers and load a crop right at the farm, so the South never needed to build big port cities the way New England did.

How people made money

Farming was how people made money in the South. Some farms were small. But the big money came from , which were very large farms that grew one crop to sell.

Tobacco was the main crop in Virginia and Maryland. It was worth so much that people sometimes paid with tobacco instead of coins. Rice grew in South Carolina and Georgia, and indigo (a plant used to make deep blue dye) grew there too. All of these were . They were grown to be sold, not eaten, and most of them were to Europe.

Slavery had no end date, and by law a child born to an mother was too. By the 1700s, people did most of the work on Southern , and in South Carolina more than half of all the people were .

Who settled here, and why

Most people did not come to the South for religion. They came for land and money. Some came to escape debts they owed in England, and Georgia was started in 1732 partly as a place for them.

Georgia also banned slavery when it began. Then planters there asked for the ban to be removed, and in 1751 it was. That matters, because it shows slavery was something people chose, not something the weather decided.

Africans did not come by choice at all. They were captured, sold and carried here by force. That is the difference that matters most on this page.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor connected to Britain and an elected assembly of colonists. The assemblies (groups of elected men who made laws for a colony) were controlled by rich owners, and the laws they wrote protected rich owners. That is still — but only for some people.

So what?

The warm weather and rich soil of the South made possible, and made money. Growing them the way planters wanted meant a huge amount of year-round work, and planters took that work from people. The land shaped the economy, but people chose how to get the labor.
