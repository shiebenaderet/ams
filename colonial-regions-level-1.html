The Three Colonial Regions

Words you need

The same words at every level. The last three belong to one region each.

Cash crop
A plant grown to sell for money, not to eat.
Subsistence farming
Growing just enough food to feed your own family.
Indentured servant
Someone who worked for about 4 to 7 years to pay for the trip across the ocean. Then they were free.
Enslaved
Forced to work for no pay, with no right to leave. Enslaved people were treated as property that could be bought and sold.
Export
Something a place sells to another country.
Self-government
People making their own local rules instead of taking orders from far away.
Puritan New England
A member of a religious group that wanted to live exactly by the Bible.
Tolerance Middle
Letting people believe and worship differently from you.
Plantation Southern
A very large farm that grew one crop to sell, worked by enslaved people.

New England Colonies

Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire

The land and the weather

The New England colonies were Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Hampshire. The soil there is thin and full of rocks. Glaciers (huge sheets of slow-moving ice) dragged those rocks across the land during the ice age and left them behind. Winters are long and cold. The growing season is short.

That means no cash crop would grow here. But New England had two other things: a coast full of deep, safe harbors (sheltered water deep enough for ships to anchor safely), and forests full of tall trees.

How people made money

Since farming could not make them rich, New Englanders made money from the sea. They caught cod, dried it, salted it, and sold it to other countries. They cut down trees and built ships. New England ships were some of the best in the world. Boston became a busy port where goods came in and went out.

Most families still farmed. They grew corn, beans, and squash to feed themselves. That is subsistence (growing just enough to feed your own family) farming. It fed you. It did not make you rich.

Who did the work

Most of the work was done by the family that owned the farm. Children worked as soon as they were old enough. Neighbors helped each other with big jobs like raising a barn.

Some workers were indentured servants. Some people in New England were enslaved, though far fewer than in the South. New England merchants also made money from ships that carried enslaved people and the goods slavery produced.

Who settled here, and why

Most people came to New England for religion. The Puritans (English Protestants who wanted a stricter, purer church) wanted to worship in their own way and build a town that followed their rules. They came in families, not alone. That is one reason New England towns grew fast.

Who made the rules

Britain sent a governor. But New England towns ran most things themselves in town meetings, where men who owned land voted on local questions.

Before the Pilgrims even left their ship in 1620, they signed the Mayflower Compact, promising to make fair laws together. That is an early piece of self-government.

So what?

New England’s land was bad for farming, so its people turned to the ocean instead. Fish, ships, and trade made the money that the soil could not. Their religion shaped how they lived, and their town meetings started an idea that would matter later: people making their own rules.

Middle Colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware

The land and the weather

The Middle Colonies were New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey and Delaware. They sat between cold New England and the hot South, and their weather sat in between too: warm summers, cold winters, and a growing season long enough to grow a lot.

The soil was deep and good. Two big rivers, the Hudson and the Delaware, ran from the farmland out to the ocean. That mattered. A farmer could load grain on a boat and it would reach a ship.

How people made money

The Middle Colonies grew so much wheat that people called them the breadbasket (a region that grows most of the grain everyone else eats) of America. Farmers grew wheat, rye, oats and barley. They ate some of it and sold the rest. Grain was their cash crop.

They also made things. People built ships, made iron tools, and cut lumber. Skilled workers made shoes, furniture and barrels by hand. New York City and Philadelphia grew into big ports where goods came in and went out.

Who did the work

Most farms here were family farms, and the family did the work. Farmers also hired workers and paid them.

Many people came as indentured servants, especially from Germany and Ireland. They worked a few years to pay for the trip, then they were free and could get land. Some people in the Middle Colonies were enslaved, especially in New York City. But there were no huge plantations here, because wheat does not need hundreds of workers the way rice does.

Who settled here, and why

People came here from many countries: England, Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden, Scotland and Ireland. They spoke different languages and belonged to different churches.

Pennsylvania was started by William Penn, a Quaker. Quakers (a religious group who refused to fight in wars) believed in peace and fairness. A Pennsylvania law in 1701 said anyone who believed in God could worship their own way. That tolerance is why so many different people moved there.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor sent from Britain and an elected assembly of colonists who made local laws. Colonists here had a real say in their own rules, which is self-government.

So what?

The Middle Colonies had good farmland and good ports, so they got both a farming economy and a city economy. Because they welcomed people from many places and many religions, they became the most mixed region in the colonies.

Southern Colonies

Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia

The land and the weather

The Southern Colonies were Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina and Georgia. The weather there was hot in summer and mild in winter. The growing season was long. Crops could grow for most of the year.

The land was flat and the soil was rich. Wide, slow rivers ran through it out to the ocean. A ship could sail up one of those rivers and load a crop right at the farm.

How people made money

Farming was how people made money in the South. Some farms were small. But the big money came from plantations, which were very large farms that grew one crop to sell.

Tobacco was the main crop in Virginia and Maryland. It was worth so much that people sometimes paid with tobacco instead of coins. Rice grew in South Carolina and Georgia. Indigo (a plant used to make deep blue dye), a plant that makes blue dye, grew there too. All of these were cash crops. They were grown to be sold, not eaten, and most of them were exported (sold and shipped out to another country) to Europe.

Who did the work

Cash crops like tobacco and rice need people working almost every month of the year. That is why the South’s labor system looks the way it does.

At first, much of the work was done by indentured servants from England. They worked about four to seven years and then went free. Over the 1600s, landowners moved to enslaved African workers instead. Enslaved people were not paid, could not leave, and were treated as property. Slavery had no end date, and by law a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved too. By the 1700s, enslaved people did most of the work on Southern plantations.

Who settled here, and why

Most people did not come to the South for religion. They came for land and money. Some came to escape debts they owed in England.

Enslaved Africans did not come. They were captured, sold and carried here by force. That is the difference that matters most on this page.

Who made the rules

Each colony had a governor connected to Britain and an elected assembly of colonists. The assemblies (groups of elected men who made laws for a colony) were controlled by rich plantation owners, and the laws they wrote protected rich plantation owners. That is still self-government — but only for some people.

So what?

The warm weather and rich soil of the South made cash crops possible. Cash crops made money. Growing them the way planters wanted meant a huge amount of year-round work, and planters took that work from enslaved people. The land shaped the economy, but people chose how to get the labor.
